# Interiors — Naruko

## Club Carcosa

The yellow marquee stutters over the canal like a held breath that keeps
forgetting to finish, three letters lit, two dark, then the reverse, a
code the regulars have stopped trying to read and started trusting
instead. Inside, the ceiling is low and water-stained the color of weak
tea, and the walls sweat faintly with the damp that never fully leaves a
building half-sunk in a side canal. The stage faces the sea — not the
room, the sea — a wall of tall salt-fogged windows behind the drum riser
so that whoever plays here plays with their back to the audience and
their face to the glowing water, and nobody has ever complained about
this, because the regulars come as much to watch the band watch the tide
as to hear them play.

The house setlist runs the same six songs most nights, hard rock bent
around ambient hush the way a fist relaxes into an open hand:

1. **"Concrete Halo"** — driving, distorted, four-on-the-floor, the
   opener that clears the doorway fog out of everyone's ears.
2. **"Gymnopédie No. 1" (Satie, house arrangement)** — the first
   between-set hush, played nearly straight, just a little reverb added
   like salt water in the wound.
3. **"Static Bride"** — the loudest song of the night, feedback used as
   a second vocalist.
4. **"Gnossienne No. 3" (Satie, house arrangement)** — the second hush,
   slower than the first, timed to when the crowd has started to sit
   down at the tables instead of standing at the stage.
5. **"Yellow Coat, Empty Room"** — a ballad, named for the back room, the
   closest thing Club Carcosa has to a hit.
6. **"Last Broadcast"** — the closer, hard rock again but tired now,
   fading out rather than ending, so the silence afterward always feels
   like the song is still choosing whether to stop.

Past the bar, behind a door with no sign, is the back room — smaller than
people expect, close and warm, more closet than lounge. A single garment
hangs there on a bent brass hook: a coat, tattered pale yellow, its hem
frayed into something like tally marks. Nobody remembers who left it and
nobody has taken it, not out of fear exactly, more the way you don't
finish a stranger's drink even after they've clearly left for good. The
staff dust around it. The regulars have learned, without discussing it,
to leave one seat at the back-room table open, facing the hook, in case
whoever it belongs to comes back for last call.

## The Wired Arcade

Down in the sunken gallery, the air smells of ozone and dust settled on
warm glass. Rows of CRT cabinets stand shoulder to shoulder in the dark,
each one a small colored fire — green, amber, the sickly blue of a dead
channel — and between games, in the gaps where no one is playing, the
speakers still murmur: dial-up hymns, the old modem handshake slowed down
until its shriek resolves into something almost like a choir, a sound the
district has long since stopped hearing as noise and started hearing as
liturgy. Regulars call it evensong. Nobody built it that way on purpose;
it happened because nobody ever turned the machines off.

Five cabinets remain lit and playable, their marquees hand-repainted more
than once:

- **Tideglass Run** — dodge the drifting packet-plankton washing in off
  the seawall while racing a raft down an ever-lengthening canal.
- **Nine Bells, One Wrong** — a rhythm game built on the Altar's tone;
  miss the beat and the whole screen resyncs like Naruko rain.
- **Cathode Séance** — point the cabinet's light-gun at flickering
  shapes in analog snow before they resolve into something you don't
  want fully in focus.
- **Debug Cat** — a stealth game where you play the district's watching
  cats, freezing mid-stare to avoid being noticed by anything else.
- **Boomtown Rampage** — the crossover cabinet, gleaming and slightly
  out of place among the others, a stomping city-leveling brawler
  imported wholesale from some brighter, louder world; kaiju-scaled and
  cheerfully violent, it draws the only real laughter the arcade ever
  hears, and the regulars love it a little for how loudly it refuses to
  be sad.

No one runs the arcade, exactly. The machines pay for their own upkeep in
coins nobody remembers dropping, and the lights stay on long after the
last player has gone home to sleep off the static ringing in their ears.
